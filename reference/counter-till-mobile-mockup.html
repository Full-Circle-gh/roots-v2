Counter till, on a phone

The tablet till has two columns. A phone has room for one, so the order stops being a column and becomes a bar you can open — and the products get the screen back.

Drawn at 390×844 CSS pixels, the tight case; a 412×915 phone gets the same layout with more room. Colour is the live --td-* block copied verbatim from retail-admin.css; type is Inter throughout, tabular figures on every number. English only, per the mockup rule. Companion to counter-till-mockup.html, which draws the 9.7″ tablet — this is not that screen made narrower.

ProblemWhat the shipped phone layout does today

The rail was stacked under the products and capped, so the order list became a two-line strip above the total — present, but not readable. The customer control kept a full-width row it had not earned. Both are fixed below.

5 · Settle. A takeover, not a sheet — the cashier turns the phone round or leans over it, and nothing of the till should stay readable behind. The amount is the largest thing on the screen because reading it aloud and keying it into a terminal is the entire job. Back sits top-left and never scrolls away: a standalone PWA on iOS has no swipe-back, so it is the only way out.

NotesDecisions a reviewer will ask about

  1. Why a bar and not a stacked column. A phone has one column of room and two things competing for it. The products are what the cashier touches dozens of times a sale; the order is what they check two or three times. Splitting the screen served the rare job at the cost of the constant one, and made both cramped. The bar keeps the only part of the order that must always be true — the total — and puts the rest one tap away at full height.
  2. The tile count is not decoration. With the order list closed by default, the product tile is the only confirmation that a tap registered. The count badge is what makes the closed state safe; without it a cashier would have to open the sheet after every item, which is the stacked layout again with extra steps.
  3. Customer costs one button, not a row. Walk-ins are anonymous and the control is optional, so it gets space proportional to how often it is used. It also moves into the sheet: attaching a customer is part of composing the order, not part of browsing products.
  4. A closed counter is shown, not hidden. Filtering closed stores out of the picker is defensible and was what shipped, but from the cashier's side an absent row and a broken button are the same thing. Listing it with Counter closed costs one row and answers the question before it is asked. The row is not selectable — the server refuses those sales anyway.
  5. Steppers on the row, not behind a selection. The tablet reveals quantity controls when a line is selected, which is right when a rail is always visible and space is tight. In a sheet opened on purpose there is room to show them, and a correction becomes one tap instead of three.
  6. What is unchanged. Fulfilment is always Counter and no shipping mode is shown. Payment is manual: the cashier reads the total aloud and keys it into a separate terminal, then records which wallet took it. Receipt printing stays automatic and fire-and-forget; reprint is a secondary affordance on the completed screen, not drawn here.
  7. The tablet is untouched. This layout applies below the two-column floor only. counter-till-mockup.html remains the specification at 9.7″ and above, and the two share every token, string and rule.
