The picture of all of them with A

You asked for every product, or the top 20, or a number you pick, together on one graph. One line per product does not survive that. Eight lines already cross. Twenty is the same picture, denser, and a full catalogue is a knot. The bar row is the same ranking as A, drawn so the lengths compare, and the count is yours: 10, 20, 50, or every product that sold.

Why it fails
  • No line can be followed across the week.
  • Twenty products needs twenty colors, and the screen does not have them.
  • “All” on a real catalogue is a few hundred lines.
  • The shape of one product already has a home: tap the row.

What I would build

Each product is one bar. The bar is this window. The mark is where the bar ended in the window before. A mark sitting past the end of the bar is a product that sold less. You pick how many, and the longest bar is always the biggest in that set. Money or Units changes what the length means, same switch as the list.

How the count works
  • 20 is the default. 10, 50, and All are the same picture, longer or shorter.
  • All means every product that sold. You scroll. The chart does not get a new kind of drawing.
  • Ginger’s mark sits past the bar. Quinoa’s does too. Oat milk’s mark is far behind it.
  • This sample has eight products, so 20 and All look the same here.
  • On the phone the rows are these rows. Twenty of them scroll.

Lines, only for a few you pick

This is the combined line, kept small on purpose. Tick up to five products on the list. Those five get one chart, so you can compare their shape across the days. It does not try to hold the top 20.

When this earns its place
  • Oat milk climbs through the week. Ginger shot slides. The bars cannot say that.
  • You choose the products, so the lines stay few enough to name.
  • It is a second step, after the bars. The bars answer “who, and by how much.”
